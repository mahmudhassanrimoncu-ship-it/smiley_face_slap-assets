## Chapter 01: The Question

10. The world of fan edits is enormous, and almost all of it is made by individuals.

## Chapter 02: Before The Feed

18. Fan editing is older than YouTube.
19. Anime music videos, or AMVs, were already circulating in fan communities in the 1990s, cut together on home computers. _[VERIFIED [2]]_
20. By 2009, the hub site AnimeMusicVideos.org listed well over a hundred thousand of them. _[VERIFIED [3]]_
21. The most important tool in this story is older still: After Effects first shipped in January 1993. _[VERIFIED [4]]_
22. It was built by a small company called CoSA, which Aldus bought that same year. Adobe followed in 1994. _[VERIFIED [5][6]]_
23. For a long time, compositing at that level was associated mainly with professional studios. _[OBSERVED]_
24. Then the plugins arrived. In July 2012, Video Copilot released Element 3D, bringing real-time 3D objects into After Effects. _[VERIFIED [7]]_
25. And tutorials turned techniques that once took years to discover into lessons anyone could follow. _[OBSERVED]_
26. This was the widescreen era: horizontal video, longer montages, and audiences who found edits through search and subscriptions. _[OBSERVED]_

## Chapter 03: The Vertical Turn

27. Then the screen stood upright.
28. Short vertical video exploded, and serious editing moved onto the phone.
29. Alight Motion reached the iPhone App Store in December 2019, bringing keyframes and layers to mobile. _[VERIFIED [8]]_
30. In 2020, ByteDance released internationally the editor that became CapCut. _[VERIFIED [9]]_
31. On the 14th of September 2020, YouTube announced Shorts, launching it first as a beta in India. _[VERIFIED [10][11]]_
32. A new grammar spread through this vertical world: the velocity edit.
33. Speed ramps that slow a moment down, then snap it forward on the beat.
34. Shakes, flashes, zoom transitions, and tight synchronisation with the music.
35. The editors who mastered that grammar did something important: they made it the shared language of the community. _[OBSERVED]_
36. Everything that came next was built on top of it.

## Chapter 04: The Old Grammar

37. So let's look at how that grammar actually works.
38. A velocity edit is a time remap: the clip's speed changes inside the shot, not between shots.
39. On a speed graph, the curve drops into slow motion, holds, then accelerates sharply into the next beat.
40. The shake is a separate layer of motion: fast, decaying offsets in position and rotation, often with motion blur.
41. A flash or zoom transition hides the cut at its fastest frame, so the eye reads one continuous movement.
42. All of it is locked to the audio. The beat decides where the energy lands.
43. Done well, this is real craft. A ramp that feels right is the result of many small decisions.
44. But notice what it mostly transforms: time and motion, rather than the image itself. _[HYPOTHESIS]_
45. That distinction matters for what happened next.

## Chapter 05: Money And The Swipe

46. Before looking at the new editors, look at the platform they grew up on.
47. On the 1st of February 2023, YouTube began sharing advertising revenue from Shorts with creators in its Partner Program. _[VERIFIED [12][13]]_
48. Creators keep 45 percent of the revenue allocated to them, based on their share of Shorts views. _[VERIFIED [14]]_
49. Shorts analytics also show the metric we started with: viewed versus swiped away. _[VERIFIED [1]]_
50. On the 15th of October 2024, Shorts were extended to three minutes for square and vertical uploads. _[VERIFIED [15]]_
51. In March 2024, YouTube said Shorts were averaging 70 billion daily views. _[VERIFIED [16]]_
52. By June 2025, YouTube's CEO Neal Mohan said the figure was over 200 billion. _[VERIFIED [17]]_
53. These are the company's own numbers, not independent audits. _[NOTE]_
54. But the direction is clear: an enormous, paid, swipe-driven stage for short edits.
55. A stage like this may reward something specific: the first second, and a reason to watch again. _[HYPOTHESIS]_

## Chapter 07: Anatomy Of A Modern Edit

68. What does a modern, high-density edit actually contain?
69. Here is a generic example, built for this film. It is not taken from any creator. _[NOTE]_
70. At the bottom: the source footage, upscaled and colour graded.
71. Above it, the subject, cut out from the background with a mask or rotoscope, so it can be separated in depth.
72. Behind the subject, typography. It is huge, kinetic and partly hidden, so the subject appears to stand in front of the words.
73. A 3D camera moves through these layers. Because each one sits at a different depth, they move at different speeds. That is parallax.
74. Then light: glows, flares and light leaks that make separate layers feel like one image.
75. Particles, grain and texture fill the empty space.
76. Finally, motion blur and velocity ramps tie the whole composition to the rhythm of the track.
77. Collapse the stack, and it reads as a single image moving through space.
78. This is the real shift: the edit is no longer only cutting footage. It is building new images out of it. _[HYPOTHESIS]_
79. That takes compositing, masking, 3D, typography and timing: skills from very different disciplines.

## Chapter 08: Same Problem, Two Answers

80. To make the difference concrete, give two approaches the same problem: get from shot A to shot B on a drop in the music.
81. Approach one: a speed ramp into a zoom, a flash on the beat, and a shake on landing.
82. It works. It is fast, it is readable, and it hits instantly.
83. Approach two: a mask follows the subject's edge, the camera pushes through it, and shot B is revealed already in motion.
84. Type wipes across in depth, and the curve is shaped in the graph editor so the move eases in and snaps out.
85. Look at the motion curves. A linear move looks mechanical. A shaped curve has weight.
86. Neither approach is wrong. But the second needs far more planning, and it is much harder to copy. _[HYPOTHESIS]_
87. These are generic demonstrations. We are not claiming that any of the six channels uses these exact techniques. _[NOTE]_

## Chapter 09: The Numbers And Their Limits

88. So, do the numbers show the new generation winning?
89. The honest answer is that the public numbers are messier than they look.
90. For Slicx7, vidIQ showed about 1.52 million subscribers in February 2025. Another tracker lists 4.59 million, with no date. _[VERIFIED [23][24]]_
91. For BroxEditZ, vidIQ showed 2.78 million in April 2025. A Social Blade listing showed about 3.7 million, along with a notice that it could no longer collect data. _[VERIFIED [21][20]]_
92. For Heisty, the only YouTube figure we found is an estimate claiming more than a million. The TikTok and Snapchat profiles show entirely different audiences. _[VERIFIED [30][31][29]]_
93. These figures come from different dates, different trackers and even different platforms, and some may not refer to the same channel. _[NOTE]_
94. So this film will not rank these creators. The evidence does not support a ranking.
95. What we can say is narrower: by vidIQ's figures, BroxEditZ passed two million subscribers less than three years after joining. _[VERIFIED [21]]_
96. And none of these numbers can tell us why. Subscriber counts do not measure editing complexity.
97. To show that complex editing causes growth, you would need to compare many channels, upload by upload, controlling for subject, timing and frequency.
98. No one has published that study. For now, it remains a hypothesis. _[HYPOTHESIS]_

## Chapter 10: Why The Landscape Changed

99. If complexity alone cannot explain the shift, what can?
100. Here are five explanations. Each one is a hypothesis with supporting evidence, not a proven fact. _[HYPOTHESIS]_
101. One: the stage changed. Shorts became a paid, swipe-ranked feed, and newer editors built their style for that feed from the start. _[HYPOTHESIS]_
102. Two: subject gravity. Football and blockbuster film already have vast audiences, and edits about them can ride that attention. _[HYPOTHESIS]_
103. Three: the name becomes the style.
104. Search YouTube and you will find tutorials titled 'Heisty like', 'Eagle Stan like', '10Mov like', 'Fictic like' and 'Slicx7 like'. _[VERIFIED [36][37][38][39][40]]_
105. When other editors teach your look by name, your look has become a brand.
106. Four: diffusion. Many of those tutorials recreate After Effects styles in mobile apps like Alight Motion and CapCut. _[VERIFIED [37][38][39][40]]_
107. Once a style can be copied on a phone, it stops being rare, and its originators are pushed to go further. _[HYPOTHESIS]_
108. That is an arms race. Every signature move has a shorter shelf life. _[HYPOTHESIS]_
109. Five: leverage. Scripts, presets and AI tools let one editor do work that once needed far more time. _[HYPOTHESIS [41]]_
110. Put together, the answer starts to look different.
111. The new generation did not only edit better. They edited for a different stage, about subjects with huge audiences, in styles that became recognisable brands. _[HYPOTHESIS]_
112. Complexity is part of the story. It may not be the whole story.

## Chapter 11: The Floor And The Ceiling

113. This creates a strange paradox about the barrier to entry.
114. The floor has never been lower. A phone app and a free preset can produce a convincing velocity edit. _[OBSERVED]_
115. But the ceiling has never been higher. A top-tier composition can demand After Effects, plugins, an AI upscaler and a powerful computer. _[OBSERVED]_
116. So it is easier than ever to start, and harder than ever to stand out. _[HYPOTHESIS]_
117. For established editors, this is not a story about losing talent.
118. The grammar they built is now everywhere. That is exactly what made it ordinary. _[HYPOTHESIS]_
119. Some audiences moved to new formats. Some editors moved with them. Others kept a style they still believe in.
120. A drop in engagement can reflect format changes, recommendation shifts, upload pauses or audience fatigue, not only quality. _[HYPOTHESIS]_
121. Without each channel's own analytics, no outsider can say which of those applies.

## Chapter 12: What Comes Next

122. So where does editing go from here?
123. In June 2025, YouTube said it would bring Google's Veo 3 video-generation model into Shorts. _[VERIFIED [18]]_
124. From this point on, everything is speculation. _[SPECULATION]_
125. If generated footage becomes cheap, visual density may stop being rare, just as velocity edits did. _[SPECULATION]_
126. The scarce resource could shift again, from effects to taste: the choice of moment, music and meaning. _[SPECULATION]_
127. The next generation might be defined not by how many layers they stack, but by what they choose to leave out. _[SPECULATION]_

## Conclusion

128. So, did editing evolve so far that the old standard stopped being enough?
129. Partly, yes. The craft genuinely expanded, from manipulating time to constructing images.
130. But the stage changed too: vertical feeds, paid Shorts, swipe metrics and global fandoms.
131. The new generation mastered the craft and the stage at the same time.
132. The older generation built the language they are speaking.
133. Neither story makes sense without the other.
134. The standard did not just rise. It moved.
135. This has been VINIXEDZ.
