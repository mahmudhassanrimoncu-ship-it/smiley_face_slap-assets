Welcome

Vectors turn geometry into algebra

A vector theorem like “the three medians of a triangle add up to zero” looks like it needs a clever picture. It doesn’t. Once you can translate a figure into vector language, almost every proof becomes a few lines of ordinary algebra.

That arrow is a vector. Drag its head around. Every position of the head gives a different vector, and the readout shows the two numbers that describe it. By the end of this class those two ideas, an arrow and a pair of numbers, will let you prove theorems without drawing anything accurately.

Lesson 1 of 8

A vector is a journey, not a place

A vector records two things: how far, and in which direction. It does not care where the journey starts.

A scalar is just a size: 5 kg, 20 °C, 3 metres. A vector has a size and a direction: “3 metres north-east”. We draw it as an arrow. The length of the arrow is its magnitude, written |AB|, and the arrowhead shows its direction.

Here is the idea that most people miss at first. Move the start point C below. The amber arrow CD starts somewhere else, but it has the same length and the same direction as AB. So it is the same vector.

Key idea

Two vectors are equal when they have the same magnitude and the same direction. Position does not matter. That is why we can slide a vector anywhere we like, which is exactly what we will do to add vectors.

A few names you will meet

Zero vector 0: the journey of length zero, like AA. It has no direction.
Unit vector: any vector of length 1.
Negative of a vector: same length, opposite direction. BA = −AB.

Look at a parallelogram ABCD. The side from A to B and the side from D to C are parallel, equally long and point the same way. So AB = DC, even though they are different line segments. This single fact proves a surprising number of theorems.

Lesson 2 of 8

Adding vectors: one journey after another

If you walk along a and then along b, where do you end up? That end point, measured from where you started, is a + b.

Triangle law. Put the tail of b at the head of a. The arrow from the very start to the very end is the sum. In letters this is beautifully simple:

AB + BC = AC

The inner letters match (B and B) and they “cancel”, leaving the outer letters. You will use this chain rule constantly.

Parallelogram law. Draw a and b from the same point and complete the parallelogram. The diagonal from that point is a + b. Drag the heads below and notice that going “a then b” and “b then a” land in the same place. So a + b = b + a.

Closing the loop

Chain several vectors head to tail. If the chain comes back to where it started, the total journey is zero. For a triangle:

AB + BC + CA = AA = 0

Drag the five corners below into any shape you like, even a crossed one. The total never moves from zero. Then tick the box to leave out the last side: the other four add up to AE, a shortcut straight from start to finish.

Key idea

Any closed chain of vectors adds up to 0. Turn it around and it becomes a test: if you can show some vectors fit together head to tail into a closed polygon, their sum is zero. This is the picture behind the medians theorem (T1).

Lesson 3 of 8

Subtraction: the arrow between two heads

Subtracting a vector means adding its negative: b − a = b + (−a). The picture is much simpler than the definition.

Draw a and b from the same point O. Then b − a is the arrow that goes from the head of a to the head of b. Check it with the chain rule: a followed by (b − a) gets you to the head of b, so a + (b − a) = b.

Now give the heads names. If OA = a and OB = b, then the arrow from A to B is

AB = AO + OB = −a + b = b − a
Key idea · memorise this

AB = (where you end) − (where you start) = b − a.
Getting the order backwards is the single most common mistake in vector proofs. Say it out loud: head minus tail.

Lesson 4 of 8

Scaling: stretch, shrink, flip

Multiplying a vector by a number λ stretches it by a factor |λ|. A negative λ also turns it around.

Move the slider. When λ = 2 the green arrow is twice as long as a. When λ = ½ it is half as long. When λ is negative it points the opposite way. Whatever you do, λa stays on the same line as a.

|λa| = |λ| · |a|

The parallel test

Since scaling never changes the line, we get the most useful test in the subject:

Key idea

u is parallel to v exactly when u = λv for some number λ.
Three points A, B, C lie on one straight line (are collinear) exactly when AB = λ AC. The two arrows are parallel and share the point A, so they must lie on the same line.

Scaling behaves like ordinary multiplication: λ(a + b) = λa + λb, and (λ + μ)a = λa + μa. So you can expand brackets and collect terms exactly as in school algebra. The one thing you can never do is divide by a vector.

Lesson 5 of 8

Position vectors and the section formula

Pick any point O and call it the origin. Now every point P gets a name that is a vector: p = OP. This is the bridge from geometry to algebra.

The position vector of a point is the journey from the origin to that point. Write it with the matching lower-case letter: A has position vector a, B has b, and so on. Lesson 3 already gave us the rule AB = b − a.

Where is a point that splits AB in a ratio?

Say P lies on AB with AP : PB = m : n. Then P is mm+n of the way from A to B:

p = a + mm+n(b − a)    = (m+n)a + mb − mam+n = na + mbm + n

Notice the weights cross over: m (the part next to A) goes with b. That makes sense. If P is much closer to B, then m is big, and b should dominate.

Try moving O. The position vectors all change, but P does not move. The formula works from any origin, which is why proofs can say “take any origin O” and never mention it again.

Key idea · the section formula

P divides AB internally in the ratio m : n ⇒ p = na + mbm + n.
Midpoint (ratio 1 : 1): m = a + b2.
External division in ratio m : n: p = mb − nam − n.

Lesson 6 of 8

Components: every vector is a pair of numbers

Put the origin at the corner of a grid. Two tiny arrows, i one step right and j one step up, can build every vector in the plane.

To reach the head of a, go x steps along i and then y steps along j. So a = xi + yj, often written as the pair (x, y). The two legs and the arrow form a right-angled triangle, so Pythagoras gives the length.

|a| = √(x² + y²) unit vector along a: â = a|a| (x₁i + y₁j) + (x₂i + y₂j) = (x₁+x₂)i + (y₁+y₂)j
Key idea

In components, adding and scaling is just adding and scaling the numbers. Use components for calculations (lengths, unit vectors, checking a specific case). Use letter vectors like a, b for proofs, because a proof must work for every triangle, not one with particular coordinates.

Lesson 7 of 8

The secret weapon: comparing coefficients

Take any two vectors a and b that are not parallel. Every point in the plane can be reached as xa + yb in exactly one way.

The faint lines below form a slanted grid built from a and b. Drag P anywhere: the readout finds the unique pair (x, y). Then drag b until it lines up with a. The grid collapses and most points become unreachable. That is why “not parallel” matters.

Why it is true

Suppose xa + yb = 0 with x ≠ 0. Then a = −yxb, which says a is parallel to b. That contradicts our assumption, so x = 0, and then yb = 0 forces y = 0.

Key idea · comparing coefficients

If a and b are non-zero and not parallel, then
xa + yb = x′a + y′b  ⇒  x = x′ and y = y′.
One vector equation becomes two number equations. Use it whenever two lines cross and you need to find where.

A first taste: the diagonals of a parallelogram

Take A as origin, AB = b, AD = d, so C is at b + d. Let the diagonals meet at X.

X is on AC, so x = s(b + d) = sb + sd for some s.
X is on BD, so x = b + t(d − b) = (1 − t)b + td for some t.
Compare coefficients: s = 1 − t and s = t. So s = t = ½, and X is the midpoint of both diagonals.

Lesson 8 of 8

The dot product: a detector for right angles

Addition and scaling can prove things about parallel lines and ratios. To prove something is perpendicular, or that two lengths are equal, you need one more tool.

The dot product of two vectors is a number, not a vector:

a · b = |a| |b| cos θ   (θ is the angle between them) a · b = x₁x₂ + y₁y₂   (in components)

Think of it as “how much b goes along a, times the length of a”. The amber segment below is the shadow of b on the line of a. Drag b round until it is at right angles to a. The shadow shrinks to nothing and the dot product becomes 0.

Key idea

a · b = 0 ⇔ a ⊥ b  (for non-zero vectors).
a · a = |a|², so equal lengths can be written as equal dot products.
a · b = b · a, and brackets expand normally: (a + b) · (a − b) = |a|² − |b|².

That last line is worth remembering. It proves both the rhombus theorem (T7) and the angle-in-a-semicircle theorem (T8) almost by itself.

Toolkit

One recipe for every vector proof

Every theorem in the workshop is proved the same way. Learn these four steps and you have a method, not a bag of tricks.

  1. Name the points. Choose an origin and give each important point a position vector. Pick the origin to save work: a vertex of the figure makes that vertex’s vector 0. An arbitrary point O keeps everything symmetric.
  2. Translate the facts. Turn every given fact into a vector equation using the dictionary below. Midpoints, ratios, parallel sides.
  3. Do the algebra. Write the vector you are asked about as “head minus tail”, substitute, and simplify. Expand, collect terms, compare coefficients if two expressions describe the same point.
  4. Translate back. Read the final equation as geometry: PQ = λ RS means parallel; equal position vectors mean the same point; a zero dot product means a right angle.

Mistakes that cost marks

  • Writing AB = a − b. It is always head minus tail: b − a.
  • Comparing coefficients of vectors that might be parallel. Say “since a and b are not parallel” before you do it.
  • Dividing by a vector, or cancelling a vector from both sides of a dot product. Neither is allowed.
  • Proving a theorem with specific coordinates. That checks one example; it does not prove the general case.
  • Forgetting the final sentence. End by stating what the algebra means geometrically.

Theorem 1

The three medians add up to zero

Theorem

In any triangle ABC, let D, E, F be the midpoints of BC, CA, AB. Then AD + BE + CF = 0.

A median joins a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. Read as vectors, the three medians are three arrows. Drag the triangle into any shape. With “tip to tail” switched on, the three median vectors are copied end to end, starting at A. They always form a closed triangle, so by Lesson 2 their sum is zero.

Try it yourself first: need a nudge?

Use the recipe. Name A, B, C by a, b, c. Write d with the midpoint formula. Then AD = d − a. Do the same for the other two and add.

Proof

  1. Take any origin O and let A, B, C have position vectors a, b, c.
  2. D, E, F are midpoints, so
    d = b + c2,   e = c + a2,   f = a + b2
  3. Each median is head minus tail:
    AD = b + c2 − a,   BE = c + a2 − b,   CF = a + b2 − c
  4. Add them. Each of a, b, c appears twice with a half and once with −1:
    AD + BE + CF = 2a + 2b + 2c2 − (a + b + c) = 0
    Hence the algebraic sum of the three medians is zero. ∎
Another route

By the parallelogram law, AD = ½(AB + AC). Write all three medians this way and the six side vectors cancel in pairs, since AB + BA = 0. Try writing it out.

Theorem 2

The medians meet at one point, two-thirds of the way down

Theorem

The three medians of a triangle pass through one point G, the centroid, with position vector g = a + b + c3. G divides each median in the ratio 2 : 1 from the vertex.

Try it yourself first: need a nudge?

Do not try to find where two medians cross. Instead, take the point that divides AD in the ratio 2 : 1 and work out its position vector. Look at how symmetric the answer is.

Proof

  1. Take any origin. D is the midpoint of BC, so d = b + c2.
  2. Let G be the point dividing AD in the ratio 2 : 1. By the section formula with m = 2, n = 1:
    g = 1·a + 2·d3 = a + b + c3
  3. This answer is symmetric in a, b, c. Repeating the calculation for the point dividing BE in ratio 2 : 1 gives (b + c + a)/3, and for CF gives (c + a + b)/3. These are the same vector, so they are the same point.
  4. So all three medians pass through G, and G divides each one in the ratio 2 : 1. ∎

Two corollaries you will be asked for

  1. GA + GB + GC = (a − g) + (b − g) + (c − g) = (a + b + c) − 3g = 0.
  2. For any point P: PA + PB + PC = (a + b + c) − 3p = 3g − 3p = 3PG.

Theorem 3

The midpoint theorem

Theorem

The line joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and half as long.

Try it yourself first: need a nudge?

Put the origin at A, the vertex the two sides share. Then A’s vector is 0 and you only have two letters to deal with.

Proof

  1. Let E and F be the midpoints of AB and AC. Take A as origin, with AB = b and AC = c.
  2. Midpoints: e = 0 + b2 = ½b and f = ½c.
  3. Head minus tail:
    EF = ½c − ½b = ½(c − b) = ½ BC
  4. EF is a scalar multiple of BC, so EF ∥ BC, and |EF| = ½|BC|. ∎
Lesson from this proof

Choosing a vertex as origin is often the fastest start. It deletes one vector from every expression.

Theorem 4

The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other

Theorem

In a parallelogram ABCD, the diagonals AC and BD cut each other in half.

Drag A, B and D. C is placed automatically so ABCD stays a parallelogram. The amber and green marks are the midpoints of the two diagonals. They never separate.

Try it yourself first: need a nudge?

Translate “parallelogram” as AB = DC and rearrange. What does (a + c)/2 mean as a point?

Proof

  1. Take any origin. ABCD is a parallelogram, so AB = DC, which means b − a = c − d.
  2. Rearrange: a + c = b + d.
  3. Divide by 2:
    a + c2 = b + d2
    The left side is the midpoint of AC. The right side is the midpoint of BD.
  4. Two points with the same position vector are the same point. So the diagonals share their midpoint: they bisect each other. ∎

Lesson 7 proved this a second way, by comparing coefficients. Both proofs are correct. The one here is shorter; the coefficient method works even when you do not know the answer in advance.

Theorem 5

Midpoints of any quadrilateral form a parallelogram

Theorem (Varignon)

Join the midpoints of the sides of any quadrilateral in order. The result is a parallelogram.

This one feels like magic. Make ABCD as lopsided as you like, even let it cross over itself. The shaded shape PQRS stays a parallelogram.

Try it yourself first: need a nudge?

To show PQRS is a parallelogram it is enough to show PQ = SR. Write both with the midpoint formula.

Proof

  1. Take any origin. P, Q, R, S are the midpoints of AB, BC, CD, DA, so
    p = a+b2,  q = b+c2,  r = c+d2,  s = d+a2
  2. PQ = q − p = c − a2  and  SR = r − s = c − a2.
  3. So PQ = SR: one pair of opposite sides is parallel and equal. Therefore PQRS is a parallelogram. ∎
Bonus

Both sides equal ½AC. Each side of PQRS is parallel to a diagonal of ABCD and half its length. That is the midpoint theorem (T3) used twice.

Theorem 6

The midline of a trapezium

Theorem

In a trapezium ABCD with AB ∥ DC, the segment joining the midpoints of the non-parallel sides AD and BC is parallel to AB and DC, and its length is half the sum of their lengths.

C can only slide along the line through D parallel to AB, so the shape is always a trapezium.

Try it yourself first: need a nudge?

Translate the parallel sides as DC = λ AB with λ > 0. Find MN and try to group the terms into AB and DC.

Proof

  1. AB ∥ DC and both run the same way, so DC = λ AB for some λ > 0.
  2. M and N are the midpoints of AD and BC: m = a+d2, n = b+c2.
  3. Group the terms cleverly:
    MN = (b − a) + (c − d)2 = ½(AB + DC)
  4. Substitute DC = λ AB: MN = 1 + λ2 AB. So MN ∥ AB ∥ DC, and |MN| = ½(|AB| + λ|AB|) = ½(|AB| + |DC|). ∎

Theorem 7

The diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular

Theorem

In a rhombus ABCD (all four sides equal), the diagonals AC and BD cross at right angles.

D is kept at the same distance from A as B is, so the shape is always a rhombus. Watch the dot product of the diagonals.

Try it yourself first: need a nudge?

Origin at A, AB = a, AD = b. Write both diagonals in terms of a and b, then use the last line of Lesson 8.

Proof

  1. Take A as origin, AB = a, AD = b. It is a rhombus, so |a| = |b|.
  2. ABCD is a parallelogram, so AC = a + b and BD = b − a.
  3. Expand the dot product. The middle terms cancel because a · b = b · a:
    AC · BD = (a + b) · (b − a) = |b|² − |a|² = 0
  4. A zero dot product between non-zero vectors means they are perpendicular. So AC ⊥ BD. ∎

Theorem 8

The angle in a semicircle is a right angle

Theorem

If AB is a diameter of a circle and P is any other point on the circle, then ∠APB = 90°.

Drag A to turn and resize the diameter. Drag P around the circle.

Try it yourself first: need a nudge?

Put the origin at the centre O. If OA = a, what is OB? What do you know about |p|?

Proof

  1. Take the centre O as origin. Let OA = a. B is at the other end of the diameter, so OB = −a. P is on the circle, so |p| = |a| = radius.
  2. PA = a − p and PB = −a − p = −(a + p).
  3. Dot them:
    PA · PB = −(a − p) · (a + p) = −(|a|² − |p|²) = 0
  4. So PA ⊥ PB, which means ∠APB = 90°. ∎
Notice

T7 and T8 are the same algebra: (u + v) · (u − v) = |u|² − |v|², which is zero whenever two lengths are equal. When a problem has equal lengths and asks for a right angle, reach for this identity.

Theorem 9

A sum around a regular hexagon

Theorem

For a regular hexagon ABCDEF: AB + AC + AD + AE + AF = 3AD.

Drag A to rotate and resize the hexagon. Five arrows leave A; their total is always three times the long diagonal.

Try it yourself first: need a nudge?

Pair the arrows up. Which side of the hexagon equals AB as a vector, and starts where AE ends?

Proof

  1. In a regular hexagon opposite sides are parallel and equal, and run in opposite directions round the hexagon. So AB = ED and AF = CD.
  2. Triangle law: AE + AB = AE + ED = AD.
  3. Triangle law again: AC + AF = AC + CD = AD.
  4. Add everything: (AB + AE) + (AC + AF) + AD = AD + AD + AD = 3AD. ∎
Related fact

With the centre O as origin, opposite vertices give opposite vectors: OA + OD = 0, and so on. So OA + OB + … + OF = 0. The readout checks this too.

Practice

Prove it on your own

Cover the solution, write a full proof on paper using the recipe, then compare. If you get stuck, go back to the dictionary on the Toolkit page.

1. The points with position vectors a − 2b + 3c, 2a + 3b − 4c and −7b + 10c are A, B, C. Prove they are collinear.

Show solution

AB = (2a + 3b − 4c) − (a − 2b + 3c) = a + 5b − 7c.
AC = (−7b + 10c) − (a − 2b + 3c) = −a − 5b + 7c = −AB.
So AC = −1 · AB. The vectors are parallel and share the point A, so A, B, C are collinear (A lies between B and C, as its midpoint).

2. A, B, C have position vectors a, b, c. Find the position vector of D so that ABCD is a parallelogram.

Show solution

Parallelogram means AB = DC: b − a = c − d, so d = a + c − b.

3. D, E, F are the midpoints of BC, CA, AB in triangle ABC. Prove that for any point O, OA + OB + OC = OD + OE + OF.

Show solution

Take O as origin. Then d + e + f = ½[(b + c) + (c + a) + (a + b)] = a + b + c. That is the required equation. (It also shows triangles ABC and DEF have the same centroid.)

4. Prove that the line joining the midpoints of the diagonals of a trapezium is parallel to the parallel sides and equal to half their difference.

Show solution

Let ABCD have AB ∥ DC with DC = λ AB, 0 < λ < 1 (DC the shorter side). Midpoints of AC and BD: m = ½(a + c), n = ½(b + d).
MN = ½[(b − a) − (c − d)] = ½(AB − DC) = 1 − λ2 AB.
So MN ∥ AB ∥ DC, and |MN| = ½(|AB| − |DC|).

5. In parallelogram ABCD, E is the midpoint of AB. Prove that DE cuts the diagonal AC at a point P with AP = ⅓ AC.

Show solution

Origin A, AB = b, AD = d (not parallel). Then C is at b + d and E at ½b.
P on AC: p = tb + td.
P on DE: p = d + s(½b − d) = ½sb + (1 − s)d.
Compare coefficients: t = ½s and t = 1 − s. So ½s = 1 − s, giving s = ⅔ and t = ⅓. Hence AP = ⅓ AC.

6. Prove that the diagonals of a rectangle are equal in length.

Show solution

Origin A, AB = a, AD = b, with a · b = 0 (right angle at A). AC = a + b, BD = b − a.
|AC|² = |a|² + 2a · b + |b|² = |a|² + |b|².
|BD|² = |b|² − 2a · b + |a|² = |a|² + |b|².
Equal squares of lengths, so |AC| = |BD|.

7. G is the centroid of triangle ABC and P is any point. Prove PA + PB + PC = 3PG.

Show solution

With any origin, PA + PB + PC = (a + b + c) − 3p. Since g = (a + b + c)/3, this equals 3g − 3p = 3PG.

8. Prove that it is possible to build a triangle whose sides are equal and parallel to the three medians of a given triangle.

Show solution

By T1, AD + BE + CF = 0. Draw AD, then copy BE starting at its head, then copy CF starting at the head of that. Because the sum is 0, the chain ends where it began, so the three arrows close up into a triangle.
It is a genuine triangle, not a flat line, because no two medians are parallel (any two of them cross at the centroid G).
This is the Lesson 2 fact read backwards: a sum of zero ⇔ the vectors close up into a polygon.

You are ready when

you can do problems 4 and 5 without opening the solutions. Problem 4 tests translation and grouping (T6’s idea). Problem 5 tests comparing coefficients (Lesson 7). Those two skills are behind nearly every planar vector theorem you will be set.
